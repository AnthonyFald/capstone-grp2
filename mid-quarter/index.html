01 / WHERE WE STAND

Faster answers.
More careful claims.

Two demos and a conversation with someone who worked on Evergreen changed how we think about AI, coordination, and Requests for Information.

THE MAIN FINDING

AI can help us find and prepare information. We have not shown that it reduces avoidable RFIs.

The original 30% reduction target had no measured baseline. Our next tests will measure RFI decisions directly and count the time spent checking AI output.
Public record

A real disruption

Highline’s board report documents the May 2024 water damage at Evergreen High School and the related floor removal.

Synthetic experiments

Controlled scenarios

Our meeting recordings and daily reports were created for testing. They are not Evergreen’s internal project records.

Stakeholder experience

Actual practice

Trent McMorrow’s interview explains how coordination worked on the project. It is separate evidence, not a test of our tool.

02 / DEMO FINDINGS

Useful gains, with limits we need to measure.

These results come from the completed mid-quarter analysis and the team’s first two demo comparisons. Later software runs and model checks remain available in each demo’s research files.

What went wrong

In Trial 2, AI confused electrical confirmation with material costs, assigned deadlines incorrectly, and transcribed “pouring concrete” as “reporting.” Those mistakes could create coordination problems.

What we can claim

Preparation was faster. The weighted score rewards speed, so accuracy and completeness must also be reported separately. Neither trial measured RFI decisions.

What helped

The tool separated completed physical work from a missing approval record. Manual evaluation reported no AI false alarms in this sample.

What this does not cover

The human comparison covers these ten reports. It does not cover all 44 reports and 42 expected issues in the broader collection, and it does not establish fewer RFIs.

The 93.8% time-saving claim is unverified

The evaluation lab assigns 209 seconds to ten reports; Anthony’s presentation notes assign that time to three routine reports. Verification time was excluded. We need to reconcile the task scope and measure the complete review before reporting a time reduction.

03 / STAKEHOLDER INTERVIEW

What Trent’s experience changed.

We interviewed Trent McMorrow, a Cornerstone project engineer involved in Evergreen. His account gave us a clearer picture of the work, its constraints, and where a tool might help.

01

Find the right source, then check it

Trent uses a specification-search agent to point him toward relevant pages. He also screens subcontractor questions that the specifications or drawings can already answer. He had not observed AI reducing RFI volume, but saw value in finding conflicts earlier.

Our response: Test source retrieval before formal submission. Show the source, date, and revision; treat the AI as a search aid.

02

Recording is not an available input on every project

He said Evergreen’s owner prohibited meeting recording and AI note-taking. He did not know the reason and declined to speculate. We cannot explain that decision as a privacy or liability policy.

Our response: Prioritize authorized specifications, drawings, reports, and existing minutes. Demo 1 remains a synthetic feasibility test. Site capture would need separate permission.

03

A repair discussion is not permission to start work

Remediation involved moisture testing, changing repair plans, schedule pressure, and uncertainty over payment and responsibility. Informal huddles coordinated trades; formal meetings produced minutes. Construction Change Directives (CCDs) and RFIs helped communicate official direction.

Our response: Keep proposals, documented decisions, and authorized work distinct. AI cannot turn a discussion into approval or solve physical drying time and insurance decisions.

04

The way a team uses RFIs matters

Trent described variation across projects and design teams: some discuss a question first and use the RFI to confirm direction; others work through the question within the RFI. A lower total RFI count is not enough to show better coordination.

Our response: Score avoidable submissions, needed escalation, and earlier conflict detection separately.

04 / FEEDBACK & PERSONAL REFLECTION

Speed alone is the wrong finish line.

Class feedback: justify the comparison

Our TA asked us to justify the metrics and connect each demo to the hypothesis. The original 30% target had no baseline. Navigant’s 2013 report explains RFI volume and administrative burden, not a reduction we could expect from AI.

Tadt, Hanna, and Whited reported roughly 12% and 16% unjustified RFIs on two highway projects. Those project-specific shares are not improvement targets we can transfer to Evergreen.

Read the feedback summary ↗

Our reflection: people still have to act

We initially treated coordination mainly as an information-processing problem. The interview showed how trust, responsibility, payment, and schedule pressure affect whether people can act on information.

Our demos also made fast, polished summaries look persuasive when details were wrong. We now value visible source passages, revision dates, and approval status as much as speed. Human checking is part of the workflow, and its time must count.

05 / WHAT CHANGED

A narrower, testable thesis.

REVISED HYPOTHESIS

AI-assisted retrieval of authorized documents, followed by human verification, will reduce avoidable RFI submissions and total review time without incorrectly closing questions that need formal clarification.

Still a hypothesis. Earlier discovery of genuine conflicts is a separate possible benefit.

06 / SECOND HALF OF THE QUARTER

Test the decisions, not just the output.

Demo 3 · Planned

RFI triage

Before testing, label each question as answerable from current, authorized documents or requiring formal clarification. Include outdated documents to test whether the tool notices revision conflicts.

Compare manual and assisted reviewers on avoidable submissions, necessary-question escalation, and incorrect closures.

Demo 4 · Planned

Schedule and task coordination

Test dependencies and work assignments under human review. Check proposed changes against source records and the authority needed to act.

Compare missed dependencies, conflicts, accuracy, and total preparation and review time.
